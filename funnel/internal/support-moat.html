SC-18 · Service architecture · draft for founder review

The panel is a commodity. The person who picks up the phone is not.

Any competitor can match our hardware by Friday — same Tier-1 panels, same Deye inverter, same Shopee listing. Nobody off this island can be standing in your kitchen in Naval within the hour. Support is the only line of our offer a competitor has to move house to copy. So we stop treating it as post-sale cost, and start selling it as the product — with the hardware as the delivery mechanism.

The five-minute version

read this if you read nothing else · 6

Six ideas. If we agree on these, the rest is detail.

01

Support isn't after the sale. It is the sale.

In a scam-wary market with zero installs behind us, the buyer's real question is not "how many watts?" It is "will these people still exist in March?" The service promise is the answer, and it's the first screen of every ad.

02

One ladder, three rungs: BANTAY · BANTAY PLUS · BANTAY 24.

Named, not "Tier 3/2/1" — customers hear tier numbers backwards. Bantay = watchman. The free rung never expires and never feels like a downgrade.

03

Our fault is always free and always fast.

Paid tiers never buy a faster fix for a defect we caused — that's extortion and it ends up on Facebook. They buy speed on the things that aren't our fault, plus preventive work and a person on call.

04

We call you first.

The inverter reports to us, not to the homeowner. "You should never be the one to tell us your system is down" is a sentence no PH residential installer is currently able to say.

05

A published SLA we pay for missing.

Miss the window, the month is free. A costly signal a scammer literally cannot afford to copy — which is exactly why it works as the anti-scam proof we otherwise don't have.

06

Sold by road, not by customer.

One-hour response is a function of driving distance, so we only sell it where we already have systems nearby. Neighbors unlock each other's tier. Density becomes a moat competitors can't buy their way into.

Why this works here, and nowhere else

the Biliran argument · 1

This idea would be mediocre in Metro Manila, where a competitor is fifteen minutes away. On an island where the nearest confirmed installer is across a strait, it's structural.

The industry default

What everyone else does

  • Install, gather everyone on the terrace, do a 20-minute orientation, hand over a laminated card, take a photo.
  • Give a mobile number that may or may not be answered in eight months.
  • Silence — until something breaks.
  • When it breaks: the homeowner has to notice, diagnose it themselves, and prove it to a stranger on Messenger.
  • Then wait three weeks for a part, a technician, and a ferry.
  • Warranty is a document. Service is an afterthought. Support is a cost centre everyone quietly hopes nobody uses.

What we do instead

The disruption

  • Handover is a ceremony with an object — a box built to be opened in front of the family, and later shown to the neighbour.
  • A named engineer with a printed name, not a hotline.
  • We watch the system daily and we call them — including at month 6 and month 12 when nothing is wrong.
  • Response windows written down, printed in the contract, and paid for when we miss them.
  • Our own performance published on a scorecard page anyone can read.
  • Warranty is a clock. Service is the product. Support is the reason the ₱99,500 closes at all.

Three moats, stacked

what compounds · 3

Each layer buys time for the next. The third one is the part to show anyone who thinks this is a distraction from the panel factory.

Year 1 · sells

Trust moat

We have no track record, so we buy credibility with a promise that costs us money to break. The published SLA + penalty is the only anti-scam signal available to a company with zero installs that isn't a fabricated testimonial.

Year 2 · defends

Density moat

Our cost to serve falls as systems-per-road rises. A Cebu or Tacloban installer's cost-per-visit here is fixed by geography and can never fall. At ~15 systems per barangay we can serve a one-hour SLA profitably; they cannot serve a one-day one at any price. That makes attacking Biliran unprofitable for them.

Year 3 · unlocks Model A

Data moat

Monitoring every system we install produces the only real Biliran typhoon-and-yield dataset in existence. That dataset is precisely what the Founder OS says Model A must launch on — locally honored warranty, PH typhoon field data, service turnaround — and never on ₱/W spec parity. The support business is the road to the factory, not a detour from it.

The rule that makes it fair

the most important decision on this page

Non-negotiable design rule

If it's our fault, the clock is one hour — on the free tier — forever.

The instant a customer suspects that paying ₱600 gets a faster fix for a defect we caused, we have invented a protection racket, and one Facebook post ends us in a province this small. So the paid rungs never touch warranty or workmanship. They buy speed and attention on the things that are genuinely not our fault — plus preventive work, plus a person who already knows their roof.

Always free, always top-speed, every tier

Our fault

  • Any hardware we supplied that fails inside warranty
  • Any workmanship defect — leaks, loose racking, bad crimps, wrong breaker
  • Anything that stops or degrades production on a system we installed
  • Permit or BILECO net-metering paperwork we filed wrong
  • Anything unsafe. Full stop — this one has no clock at all, we just come.
  • Warranty claims chased with the distributor by us, not by the homeowner

What the paid rungs actually buy

Not our fault

  • "Why is my bill still high?" — usage coaching, appliance audits
  • New aircon / freezer / circuit added after install
  • Damage from their own roof, their own tree, their own rewiring
  • Cleaning, and the annual electrical check
  • Post-typhoon inspection when nothing is actually broken
  • Front of the calendar queue, and a human at 12 hours instead of 48
  • Peace of mind — which is the real thing being sold, and is allowed to be

The mechanics

what makes it a moat and not a price list · 8

Any competitor can print three tiers. These are the parts that are hard to copy.

M1

We call you first

Deye / Solis / Growatt inverters all expose cloud monitoring. One daily automated sweep, one anomaly rule, one alert to us. The homeowner's first contact about a fault is us ringing them.

Why it's a moatIt reverses the relationship the whole industry runs on. The line — "you should never be the one to tell us your system is down" — is an ad, a product spec, and a service standard in nine words.

M2

The public scorecard

A page anyone can open: tickets this month, median first reply, % of windows met, longest miss and why. Our own performance, in public, including the bad months.

Why it's a moatSaaS status-page transparency, applied to hardware service. It cannot be faked and no competitor will publish theirs. Ships only once we have real data — inventing these numbers is a hard stop.

M3

An SLA we pay for missing

Miss the PLUS window and the month is free automatically — no claim form, we credit it ourselves from the ticket log.

Why it's a moatA costly signal. A fly-by-night operator cannot afford to make this promise, so making it separates us from them in one sentence — which is exactly the job an anti-scam signal has to do.

M4

Sold by road, not by customer

BANTAY 24 is only offered where we already have ≥8 systems within a ~20-minute drive. We publish the map of unlocked barangays.

Why it's a moatThe scarcity is real, not manufactured, so we can say it out loud. It also turns customers into our sales force: to unlock their own tier, neighbours have to recruit neighbours.

M5

The Kapitbahay clause

A referral that closes pays either the ₱3,000 referral fee or six months of BANTAY PLUS free — customer's choice. Referral source logged on every lead.

Why it's a moatThe visible rooftop plus the neighbour's brownout is already our viral mechanic. This wires the subscription directly into it, and the service-credit option costs us less than cash while raising retention.

M6

One number, one name

No ticket IDs, no queue theatre, no "your reference is SR-4471". A named engineer, printed on a card in their breaker box.

Why it's a moatIn a trust market a name beats a helpdesk, and we're small enough that this is true rather than staged. It stops being true at ~200 customers — that's the point to re-design it, not now.

M7

The service book

Every visit, alert and reading logged to their private page and exportable as a PDF. The system has papers when the house is sold or refinanced.

Why it's a moatCosts nothing extra — it's a byproduct of doing the job — but it makes leaving us expensive, and it makes our installs the ones that hold resale value.

M8

The unprompted call

Month 6 and month 12, on every tier including free, we call when nothing is wrong. Three questions: is the bill where we said, anything odd, anyone asking about it.

Why it's a moatIt is the single cheapest thing on this page and no competitor does it. It also doubles as our discovery interview and our referral ask — which is how it stays inside the traction half of the time split.

The typhoon protocol

where honesty is the feature

A one-hour SLA is a lie when the bridge is closed and forty customers call at once. The storm is the exact moment the promise is tested, so it gets written down before we sell it.

Printed in the contract, on every tier:

  • When PAGASA raises Signal No. 2 over Biliran, all tier clocks suspend and one shared emergency protocol takes over.
  • Work is ordered by safety risk first, production loss second — never by who pays more. A BANTAY-free customer with an arcing conductor is ahead of a BANTAY 24 customer with a dirty array, always.
  • We post a status update on the page every 12 hours during the event, including "no crews moving today."
  • Paid tiers get their days back: any month containing a suspension is pro-rated automatically.
  • Normal clocks restart when roads reopen, and we say publicly when that was.

Hard stop — carried through from the package rules

No support tier may imply that an on-grid LIWANAG system keeps the lights on during a brownout. It does not — anti-islanding shuts the inverter down by law and by design, and that is true at ₱0/mo and at ₱2,499/mo. The "what happens in a brownout?" row stays in the comparison table, and BANTAY 24 sold to a LIWANAG customer must state in writing that it buys faster service, not backup power.

Second hard stop: no response window goes into any customer-facing document until a licensed electrician / REE is on retainer and named, with their PRC number printed. A one-hour promise backed by two founders and no licence is a liability, not a moat.

The unboxing

two moments, two objects

You asked for the Apple-packaging feeling. The insight worth stealing from Apple isn't the cardboard — it's that the box is staged for an audience. Ours has two audiences: the family, and the neighbour they will show it to. Both moments below are budgeted as marketing, not as fulfilment.

Moment 1

The Signing Folio

handed over when they pay the deposit — the most nervous five minutes of the whole relationship

Rigid navy folio, chevron mark debossed, their name printed on the cover. This is where the anti-scam work happens: everything a scammer would be vague about is printed, dated and signed in ink.

A

The contract, countersigned in ink

Both founders' wet signatures, not a scan. Costly, deliberate, and it reads as such.

B

Permit & BILECO net-metering checklist

Every step, whose job it is (ours), and the expected date. The checklist the rules already require on every quote.

C

The electrician's PRC licence, printed

Name, number, expiry. The single fastest way to separate ourselves from a Facebook-page installer.

D

"What happens next" timeline card

Real dates: survey → design approval → permit filed → install → energized → first lower bill.

E

Warranty on one page, plain language

What's covered, what's not, for how long, and who to call. Written to be understood, not to be defensible.

F

The sealed BANTAY PLUS card

Heavier stock, sealed sleeve, opened last. 90 days free, then ₱600/mo, cancel anytime. Scan to activate. This is the upsell — and the pricing experiment.

What actually justifies itThe free tier is a sales cost, not a service cost. It exists so the ₱99,500 closes.

Two things to be honest about in the pitch

1. Year one, this earns nothing. Five installs at a 40% attach rate is two subscribers — ₱1,200 a month. Anyone selling this as a revenue line is fooling themselves. Its year-one job is to raise the close rate on the ₱99,500 and to make the referral loop turn. Revenue is a year-two argument.

2. The loaner inverter is self-funding, and must stay that way. At 25 PLUS subscribers (₱15,000/mo) the subscription buys a spare 5 kW hybrid in roughly three months. Until that spare is physically in the workshop, the loaner does not appear in any customer-facing document — a promise we can't deliver is a value gap, and one of those costs more than every peso this page earns.

What I need you to decide

for the founder meeting · 5

Each one has my recommendation attached. Disagree on the record, not in the hallway.

Decision 1

Do we accept the fairness rule — our fault is always free and always fast?

It costs us margin on the free tier and it removes the most obvious upsell lever in the business.

My callAccept it, and put it in writing on the website. It is the single sentence that makes the paid tiers feel like a service instead of a hostage fee, and it is the one we'd never be able to walk back once we'd broken it.

Decision 2

Does BANTAY 24 stay residential at ₱2,499, or re-aim at livelihood/commercial?

₱2,499/mo is ₱29,988/yr — 30% of the flagship price, every year. Against the corridor a homeowner actually compares to (an electrician callout, a lost day, genset fuel), that is far outside the window.

My callRe-aim it. Sell it to businesses whose downtime costs more per day than the plan costs per month, and give homeowners the ₱6,000 annual prepay instead. Keep the ₱2,499 number — it's right for the buyer it's right for.

Decision 3

Do we publish the SLA and the penalty?

Publishing a window we miss twice is worse than never publishing one. This is a promise we have to actually keep, with two people and a motorbike.

My callPublish, but only the windows we'd hit on our worst week — 48 h and 12 h, not 24 h and 4 h. Under-promise the clock, then beat it publicly. The penalty is what makes the promise mean anything, so it ships with it or neither ships.

Decision 4

Is the Handover Box marketing spend or fulfilment cost?

₱900–1,400 per install. At five installs that is ~₱6,000 of a pre-revenue budget.

My callMarketing, and we hand-build the first five ourselves. It is cheaper than a boosted post and it is the only asset that keeps selling after we leave the property.

Decision 5

Who owns support?

The rules require a named owner per live deliverable, and an unowned service promise is how SLAs get missed in week three.

My callOne of us owns "customer & service" outright, including the month-6 calls and the scorecard, and it goes in the T5T every Friday as a number — median first reply — not as a status update.
